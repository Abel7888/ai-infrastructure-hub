In a small hospital in rural Trinidad, Colorado, an AI system just spotted a brain hemorrhage that could have been missed until it was too late. In Miami, an algorithm is preventing appointment chaos for patients with complex care plans. And across Duke Health's sprawling network, 2,500 doctors are using AI scribes that have done something unprecedented in healthcare IT: actually reduced burnout.

Two years ago, this was all hype and promise. Today, it's Tuesday.

"AI isn't a side project anymore," says Michael Archuleta, CIO at Mt. San Rafael Hospital. "It's becoming part of how modern healthcare operates—and we've moved from exploring possibilities to engineering outcomes."

HealthTech spoke with seven healthcare IT leaders who've spent the last two years neck-deep in AI pilots, rollouts, and the hard lessons that come with both. Their message: AI is delivering real results in healthcare, but the wins are coming from unexpected places, and the challenges are more nuanced than the industry anticipated.

## The Surprise Winners: Small Fixes, Big Impact

Forget the sci-fi fantasies of AI diagnosing rare diseases or predicting pandemics. The AI applications actually transforming healthcare are solving frustratingly mundane problems—and that's exactly why they're working.

**The Documentation Revolution**

Ask any doctor what they hate most about modern medicine, and "documentation" consistently tops the list. It's the administrative paperwork that keeps them from patients, burns them out, and sends many to early retirement.

Enter ambient AI scribes: systems that listen to patient visits and generate clinical notes automatically.

Dr. Bimal Desai has worked in health informatics for over 20 years at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia. He's seen countless technologies promise to fix healthcare's problems. "This is the first time I've seen a single digital intervention significantly reduce burnout," he says. "The first time."

Duke Health's numbers tell the story. They've rolled out ambient scribes to 2,500 providers generating more than 30,000 AI-assisted notes every week. The measurable impacts? Reduced burnout, higher provider satisfaction, better on-time chart closures, and improved productivity.

Dr. Eric Poon, Duke's chief health information officer, calls it "phenomenal." Based on the ambulatory success, they've expanded to emergency departments and inpatient environments.

**The Chaos Prevention You'll Never See**

Some of the most impressive AI work is completely invisible to patients—which is precisely the point.

When you reschedule a doctor's appointment, you probably don't think about the cascade of coordination required: updating multiple orders, notifying referral specialists, realigning care team schedules, ensuring insurance pre-authorizations stay valid. At Jackson Health System in Miami, that coordination used to consume significant physician and staff time while creating opportunities for dangerous errors.

Now an AI system handles it automatically.

"It's eliminated the manual reconciliation that previously consumed significant physician and staff time," says Connie Barrera, the system's CISO. The results aren't just operational—vastly reduced no-show rates mean appointments become available faster, directly improving patient care outcomes.

**Minutes That Save Lives**

In rural Colorado, Archuleta's team faces a challenge urban hospitals rarely consider: distance. When someone in Trinidad has a stroke or brain hemorrhage, the nearest specialized care might be hours away. Minutes matter.

AI-powered radiology detection now helps identify time-sensitive, life-threatening conditions—intracranial hemorrhage, vessel occlusion, pulmonary embolism, cervical spine fractures—earlier and more consistently than human eyes alone.

"This isn't theory, it's real clinical impact happening right now," Archuleta says. "I've always believed your ZIP code should never determine your healthcare outcomes. AI is one of the most powerful tools we have to make that statement real."

## The Hard Lessons: What Didn't Work

For every success story, these leaders have learned painful lessons about what doesn't work—and why.

**The "Garbage In, Garbage Out" Problem**

Crystal Broj watched it happen multiple times at the Medical University of South Carolina: organizations eager to deploy cutting-edge AI would layer it on top of broken processes, hoping the technology would somehow fix fundamental workflow problems.

It never did.

"AI layered on top of broken processes doesn't help—it just accelerates the mess," Broj says bluntly. She's completely changed her approach: "We now focus on process improvement and fully leveraging existing tools before introducing new AI technology, so automation amplifies good design rather than compensating for poor workflows."

The lesson resonates across the industry. The most successful AI implementations embed into existing workflows rather than forcing clinicians to change how they work. If your process is broken, fix it first. Then add AI.

**The Magic Wand Myth**

Dusanka Delovska-Trajkova, CIO at Ingleside, has watched leaders make a consistent mistake: treating AI as a silver bullet.

"People expect a magic wand," she observes, "but what they get is a multipurpose toolkit that still requires structure and accountable leadership."

The problem isn't technological—it's cultural. "AI can support solutions; humans must own them. That's why AI is so hard. It's not that we don't have technology. It's a culture change that takes time."

Many organizations open the AI toolbox before asking the right questions. Worse, they deploy it without the resources—money and people—needed to implement it properly.

**The Speed Trap**

Brenton Hill, who heads operations at the Coalition for Health AI, sees organizations making a dangerous mistake in their enthusiasm: moving faster than their governance can handle.

"My biggest concern is organizations moving faster than sufficient validation, monitoring, governance, or clinician engagement can keep pace," Hill warns. "AI deployed without clear governance can introduce bias and safety risks that undermine trust."

The eagerness to deploy is understandable—but rushing past essential safeguards creates risks that can set back AI adoption by years.

## The Concerns Keeping Leaders Awake

Despite the successes, these executives harbor serious concerns about where AI in healthcare is headed.

**The Black Box Problem**

"What concerns me is governance, not the technology itself," Archuleta says. "AI must never become a black box that people blindly follow. It needs transparency, validation, and clinical oversight."

Barrera worries about something specific: audit trails. "We need to demonstrate not just what the AI decided, but why, and in ways that satisfy HIPAA and other regulatory requirements." Every AI system integrated presents a new attack surface, and she's concerned about adversarial attacks—prompt injection vulnerabilities in clinical chatbots, AI systems manipulated to make harmful decisions during the critical window when security frameworks are still maturing.

**When Doctors Stop Double-Checking**

Dr. Desai identifies a subtle but potentially catastrophic risk: automation bias.

"The risk is that people won't know to question the output of AI—they stop double-checking," he explains. "With the non-zero risk of hallucination or 'garbage in/garbage out' AI summaries, I worry that errors will go unchecked and be perpetuated throughout the system."

It's related to but distinct from "deskilling"—the fear that doctors relying on AI will lose critical skills. Desai is less worried about that ("nobody laments that we've been deskilled in the use of slide rules"), but the idea that doctors might trust AI outputs without verification keeps him up at night.

**The Pricing Trap**

Broj raises a concern that's deeply practical: cost models. Many AI systems charge per interaction or per use. "If AI is priced per run or per interaction, costs can quickly scale faster than the clinical or operational savings it's meant to offset," she notes.

Health systems need predictable, fixed pricing that can be budgeted and sustained long-term. Without it, successful AI pilots can become financial nightmares when scaled across an entire organization.

**The Environmental Impact Nobody Talks About**

Dr. Desai brings up something most discussions ignore: "I have significant concerns about the environmental impact of AI and power consumption of data centers."

It's the area where health systems have the least control. They can choose vendor partners committed to green solutions, favor local models where appropriate, and develop guidelines for "appropriate" use of AI. But fundamentally, they're dependent on tech companies' commitment to sustainability.

"As tech companies develop strategies to address power consumption and environmental impact, health systems should favor vendor partners who are showing a true commitment to green solutions," Desai urges.

## How Strategies Are Evolving

The leaders who've been in the AI trenches longest have fundamentally changed how they approach it.

**From Pilots to Infrastructure**

Jackson Health System has stopped running isolated AI pilots. Instead, they're establishing comprehensive governance frameworks that treat AI systems as critical infrastructure requiring the same rigor as any other clinical system.

"Every AI proposal undergoes formal vendor risk assessment, data governance review, security architecture evaluation, and regulatory compliance validation before touching patient data or clinical workflows," Barrera explains. They're also building their own large language model to leverage their proprietary data.

**The Validation Problem**

At Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, Dr. Desai's team discovered that vendor-released AI tools require extensive clinical validation—both for clinical accuracy and for pediatric-specific considerations.

For their ambulatory AI summaries, up to 200 providers spent nearly a year validating the tool before they felt comfortable deploying it widely.

"Given the huge number of AI use cases our EHR vendor is developing, we need to think differently about how we resource the teams responsible for testing, validation, training, and deployment," Desai says. They're creating a dedicated AI evaluation core with data analytics, informatics, researchers, and clinical stakeholders—plus strong AI governance covering security, privacy, ethics, safety, and legal issues.

**Rapid Testing, Careful Scaling**

Duke Health has embraced what Poon calls "agility" in AI selection. They've developed approaches to conduct rapid evaluations of AI solutions in low-cost, low-risk ways, allowing them to quickly identify what might bring value.

But rapid testing doesn't mean rapid deployment. "For solutions that we test, we still want to make sure our clinicians or patients find them helpful and that they improve care or outcomes in measurable ways."

They're also partnering with select AI vendors to co-develop extensions of high-value solutions, keeping both the vendors and Duke at the forefront of addressing healthcare challenges.

## What's Next: The View From Here

These leaders share cautious optimism tempered by realism.

The excitement is palpable. AI-assisted computer vision is being tested to prevent falls and pressure injuries in hospitals. Agentic AI is reducing the burden of chart reviews for patient referrals and clinical registry data abstraction. Revenue cycle AI is streamlining prior authorizations and coding.

"What excites me most is AI's ability to scale human capacity, allowing care teams to focus on patients rather than process," Broj says. "We're finally seeing AI move from experimentation to real, operational impact."

For underserved areas, the promise is even greater. "For underserved healthcare providers, it brings the opportunity of hopefully being able to provide high-quality service with reduced overhead and expenses," says Jim Roeder, VP of IT at Lakewood Health System. "This would allow these places to continue to keep their doors open for their communities."

But the path forward requires discipline.

"There is still a lot of work to be done to translate the promise of generative and agentic AI into measurable benefits at the bedside and in healthcare operations," Dr. Poon acknowledges. "Healthcare leaders need to learn about the promise and limitations of AI so we can focus our limited energies on the most promising solutions."

## The Verdict

AI in healthcare has crossed a threshold. It's no longer a question of "if" but "how"—how to deploy it safely, how to govern it effectively, how to ensure it augments rather than replaces human judgment, how to make it sustainable.

The leaders on the frontlines report genuine progress: doctors with more time for patients, staff freed from soul-crushing administrative work, patients getting faster and more accurate care regardless of geography.

But they're also clear: this only works with discipline, governance, validation, and a willingness to fix broken processes before automating them.

"AI must be held to the same standard as medicine," Archuleta insists. "Safe, accountable, and continuously monitored."

Two years ago, that was aspirational. Today, the leaders making AI work in healthcare are proving it's possible—one carefully validated implementation at a time.
